# Chapter 10: The Invisible Tax Bureau That Bleeds Both Ways

September 2015. Shanghai.

Colin Huang sat in an office, looking at backend data for the Pinduoduo app.

He wore an ordinary blue shirt, his hair slightly messy. On his desk were only a laptop and a cup of green tea. Huang was not good with words, but his thinking was extremely sharp. He had worked at Google, participating in the development of the AdWords system. He knew the value of advertising and traffic deeply, and he knew the power of platforms deeply.

He was the founder of Pinduoduo. A few months earlier, he had left a gaming company to start this business. His idea was simple: sell cheap goods through social group buying.

Pinduoduo's model was: users initiate group buys, invite friends to purchase together, and once a certain number is reached, the transaction closes at an extremely low price. For example, a case of apples originally priced at 50 yuan would cost only 20 yuan for a group of 5.

This model was not new. Groupon had done group buying back in 2008. But Pinduoduo's innovation was combining group buying with social networks. Users shared group buy links through WeChat, inviting friends to participate. WeChat's social relationship chain became Pinduoduo's customer acquisition channel.

Pinduoduo expanded rapidly in third- and fourth-tier cities and rural markets. These users were price-sensitive and willing to spend time on group buying to save money. They might not know about Taobao, but they knew about Pinduoduo.

In 2016, Pinduoduo's user base exceeded 100 million. In 2018, Pinduoduo listed in the United States with a market cap exceeding $20 billion.

Huang's success was built on one fact: Taobao and JD.com had become too expensive.

**Meanwhile, on the other side of the Earth.**

2006. Ottawa.

Tobias Lütke sat in a coffee shop, writing code.

He was a programmer who had previously opened an online snowboard shop. He found that existing e-commerce platforms were not user-friendly: complex features, high fees, difficult customization. So he decided to write his own.

Using the Ruby on Rails framework, he spent two months writing a concise e-commerce system. He turned it into a SaaS product and named it Shopify.

Other shop owners discovered Shopify and started using it. Lütke realized that selling software was more promising than selling snowboards. He shifted his focus to Shopify's development.

Shopify's model was: providing e-commerce infrastructure for merchants—website templates, payment systems, logistics integration, marketing tools. Merchants pay a monthly subscription fee and can open a store on Shopify.

This model is called "SaaS" (Software as a Service). Merchants don't need to develop their own e-commerce systems; they just rent Shopify's services.

By 2020, there were over 1 million merchants on Shopify, with annual transaction volume exceeding $100 billion. By GMV, it was one of the largest U.S. e-commerce platforms after Amazon.

---

What is the essence of two-sided platforms?

It is collecting rent.

Platforms connect buyers and sellers, provide matching services for both sides, and extract commissions from it. This commission is essentially a form of "tax."

Rent collection has begun.

### The Composition of Platform Tax

Platform tax consists of multiple parts:

**Transaction Commission**: For each transaction, the platform extracts a certain percentage of commission. Taobao's commission rate is about 3%-5%, Amazon's is about 8%-15%, and Didi's is about 20%-30%.

**Advertising Fees**: To gain more exposure, sellers place ads on the platform. Taobao's Direct Train and Display Ads, Amazon's Sponsored Products are all advertising products. Advertising fees are charged per click or per impression.

**Service Fees**: The platform provides various value-added services and charges fees. For example, Taobao's store decoration and data analysis tools; Amazon's FBA (Fulfillment by Amazon) logistics service; Shopify's app store.

**Payment Fees**: The platform provides payment services and charges handling fees. Alipay, WeChat Pay, and PayPal all charge about 0.6%-3% in payment handling fees.

Combined, these fees mean that for every 100 yuan in goods sold, sellers may pay 20-30 yuan to the platform.

### Steve Jobs and the Apple Tax

In 2008, Steve Jobs stood on stage at Apple headquarters, announcing the birth of the App Store.

He wore his signature black turtleneck and blue jeans, with gray New Balance sneakers on his feet. His speech was passionate, but few noticed a detail: the App Store would charge a 30% commission.

This percentage was set by Jobs. He believed that Apple provided developers with a platform, payment system, and user traffic, and 30% was a reasonable return.

This is the "Apple Tax."

The composition of the Apple Tax is simple: when developers sell apps or in-app purchases through the App Store, Apple takes 30% by default (the Small Business Program allows 15% for developers with under $1 million in annual App Store revenue).

This percentage may not seem high, but for some industries, it is fatal. Music streaming service Spotify, game company Epic Games, and dating app Tinder have all publicly opposed the Apple Tax.

Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney was the fiercest critic. He believed that Apple was using its monopoly position in iOS to charge unreasonable commissions. In 2020, Epic Games added a direct payment feature in *Fortnite*, bypassing Apple's payment system. Apple immediately removed *Fortnite* from the App Store, and Epic Games sued Apple for antitrust violations.

This lawsuit lasted several years. The court ultimately ruled that Apple cannot prohibit developers from providing external payment links within apps, but the 30% commission itself is not illegal.

Jobs has passed away, but his 30% rule is still in effect. The Apple Tax brings Apple tens of billions of dollars in revenue annually, becoming the core of Apple's services business.

### Pony Ma and the WeChat Ecosystem

In 2011, WeChat launched. Pony Ma didn't expect that this simple instant messaging app would grow into a super platform.

Pony Ma wears glasses, speaks gently, but has extremely keen insight. He knows the value of platforms deeply and the power of ecosystems deeply. His famous quote is: "Connect everything."

WeChat's connectivity makes it the "water, electricity, and coal" of China's internet.

**WeChat Pay**: Launched in August 2013, it quickly became one of China's largest mobile payment platforms. WeChat Pay's handling fee is 0.6%, far lower than traditional payment channels.

**Official Accounts**: Launched in 2012, allowing businesses and individuals to publish content on WeChat. Official accounts changed China's content ecosystem, giving birth to the self-media era.

**Mini Programs**: Launched in 2017, allowing developers to develop lightweight apps within WeChat. Mini programs require no download or installation; they are ready to use and have become a substitute for apps.

The core of the WeChat ecosystem is "decentralization." Unlike Apple's closed ecosystem, WeChat does not charge commissions; it only provides infrastructure. Developers can freely develop mini programs; users can freely use services.

But this "decentralization" is superficial. WeChat controls the traffic entrance, controls the user relationship chain, and controls the payment system. It doesn't need to collect taxes because it controls the lifeline of the entire ecosystem.

Pony Ma's philosophy is: don't compete for short-term gains, compete for long-term momentum. WeChat is not in a hurry to monetize but first establishes the ecosystem. Once the ecosystem is established, there are many ways to monetize: advertising, financial services, enterprise services.

By 2023, WeChat's monthly active users exceeded 1.3 billion, and mini programs' daily active users exceeded 600 million. WeChat has become China's largest digital ecosystem.

### Network Effects and Lock-in

Why can platforms collect rent?

Because of network effects.

Network effects mean: the more users, the higher the platform's value. The more buyers, the more willing sellers are to join; the more sellers, the more willing buyers are to use. This is a positive cycle.

Network effects lead to "winner-takes-all." A market can usually only accommodate one or two large platforms. Taobao dominates China's e-commerce market, Amazon dominates the US e-commerce market, and Didi dominates China's ride-hailing market.

Once the winner is established, it can increase the commission rate. Sellers have no other choice but to accept. Buyers also have no other choice but to accept.

This is "lock-in." Sellers have accumulated customers, reviews, and data on the platform; migration costs are high. Buyers have accumulated membership benefits and shopping habits on the platform; migration costs are also high.

Platforms use lock-in to continuously increase commissions. This is the story of "the dragon-slayer becomes the dragon." The dragon-slayer eventually becomes the dragon.

The rent collection behavior of platforms has sparked resistance.

### Pinduoduo: The Rebellion of the Lower-Tier Market

Taobao began "consumption upgrading" in the 2010s. It supported branded merchants, cracked down on counterfeit goods, and increased average order value. This led to a large number of low-end sellers being squeezed out of Taobao.

These sellers needed a new platform. Pinduoduo welcomed them.

Pinduoduo does not charge commissions, only a 0.6% payment handling fee. This is much cheaper than Taobao. Sellers are willing to sell cheaper goods on Pinduoduo because costs are lower.

Pinduoduo also attracted first-tier city users through "10 billion subsidies." iPhones, Dyson hair dryers, Moutai liquor—after Pinduoduo's subsidies, they are hundreds of yuan cheaper than official channels.

By 2020, Pinduoduo's annual active buyers exceeded 700 million, surpassing Taobao. Pinduoduo became the e-commerce platform with the largest user base in China.

Pinduoduo's success proves that platform taxes can be challenged. If a platform's commissions are too high, sellers and buyers will look for alternatives.

The rebels have arrived.

### Shopify: Arming the Rebels

Shopify's slogan is "Arming the Rebels."

Who are the rebels? Those merchants who don't want to be ruled by Amazon.

Amazon's commissions on third-party sellers are getting higher and higher. Commissions, advertising fees, logistics fees—combined, they may exceed 30% of sales. Many sellers can't make money on Amazon.

Shopify provides an alternative: merchants can build their own independent websites on Shopify, controlling their own customers, data, and brands. No need to pay rent to Amazon.

But Shopify also has problems. Independent websites have no traffic; merchants need to drive traffic themselves. Facebook ads, Google ads, influencer marketing—all cost money. Moreover, consumers are accustomed to shopping on Amazon, and independent websites have lower conversion rates.

Shopify tried to solve this problem. It launched the Shop App, a shopping app aggregating all Shopify merchants. It also launched Shop Pay, a cross-merchant payment system.

By 2020, Shopify's market cap exceeded $100 billion, becoming the highest-valued company in Canada.

### Web3: The Escape to Decentralization

In the 2020s, the Web3 concept emerged. The core idea of Web3 is: use blockchain technology to build decentralized platforms, eliminating middleman commissions.

For example, the NFT marketplace OpenSea initially only charged a 2.5% commission, much lower than traditional art auction houses. The decentralized exchange Uniswap v2 charges a 0.3% liquidity-provider fee on trades, plus on-chain Gas fees.

But Web3 also faces challenges. The user experience of decentralized platforms is not as good as centralized platforms. Blockchain transaction speeds are slow and fees are high. Ordinary users find it difficult to understand private keys, wallets, and smart contracts.

By 2023, Web3 has not yet become mainstream. But it provides a vision: platform taxes are not inevitable; technology can change the distribution of benefits.

The rent collection ability of platforms depends on technical infrastructure.

### Cloud Computing: The Cornerstone of Platforms

Modern two-sided platforms all run on cloud computing platforms.

Taobao runs on Alibaba Cloud. Amazon is the owner of AWS, and AWS supports Amazon's e-commerce business. Didi runs on Alibaba Cloud and Tencent Cloud.

Cloud computing provides elastic computing resources. Platforms can automatically scale up or down servers based on traffic changes. This reduces operating costs and improves reliability.

Cloud computing also provides various PaaS (Platform as a Service) services: databases, message queues, machine learning platforms, CDN... Platforms don't need to develop this infrastructure themselves; they can directly use cloud services.

Cloud computing costs are a significant expense for platforms. Taobao may pay over 1 billion yuan to Alibaba Cloud annually. AWS revenue accounts for 12% of Amazon's total revenue but contributes over 60% of operating profit.

### Edge Computing: Reducing Latency

Edge computing moves computing tasks from the cloud to the network edge, closer to the user's location.

For applications requiring high real-time performance, edge computing can reduce latency. For example, Didi's dispatch algorithm needs to respond within milliseconds; edge computing can place computing closer to drivers and passengers.

Edge computing can also reduce bandwidth costs. If all data is transmitted to the cloud for processing, bandwidth costs are enormous. Edge computing can process data locally, transmitting only results to the cloud.

CDN is a form of edge computing. CDN nodes cache static content; users get content from the nearest node without accessing the origin server.

In the 2020s, edge computing began supporting more complex computing tasks. NVIDIA launched the EGX platform, which can run AI inference at the edge. This provides infrastructure for autonomous driving, industrial IoT, smart cities, and other applications.

### Shopify's Antitrust Hardware Foundation

Shopify's "Arming the Rebels" is not just a slogan; behind it is a sophisticated global infrastructure.

Shopify's global traffic is hosted by Cloudflare. Cloudflare has deployed edge nodes in over 310 cities worldwide, each node containing customized server hardware—AMD EPYC-based 1U rack servers with large-capacity NVMe SSDs for caching. When a Japanese consumer visits an American Shopify merchant's independent site, the page is returned from Cloudflare's Tokyo node with latency under 50 milliseconds. This allows independent sites to load as fast as Amazon—while Amazon has over 30 regional AWS data centers worldwide.

Shopify also developed its own Shop Pay accelerated checkout system. It pre-stores users' addresses and payment information in the backend; checkout requires only one click. Shop Pay-processed transactions are routed through Stripe's payment gateway to the global banking network—each transaction completes authorization within 350 milliseconds, passing through hardware nodes including: phone NFC/fingerprint sensor → Cloudflare edge → Shopify application server → Stripe API gateway → Visa/Mastercard clearing network → issuing bank. Six hops. Every hop is hardware.

### Web3 Mining: The Siphoning Effect on GPU Supply Chains

From 2020 to 2021, cryptocurrency mining had a devastating siphoning effect on GPU supply chains.

Ethereum mining uses GPUs. An NVIDIA RTX 3080's mining hash rate is about 95MH/s, with daily earnings of about $15-20 (at 2021 peak). Miners swept up inventory frantically, and the RTX 3080's market price soared from the official MSRP of $699 to over $2,000.

This not only affected gamers—more critically, it squeezed GPU supply for AI startups and cloud computing vendors. At that time, a Chinese AI startup wanted to purchase 1,000 A100s for training large models, and the delivery cycle was extended to 6-8 months. The mining industry used real money to snatch GPUs away from the AI industry chain.

In September 2022, Ethereum transitioned from Proof of Work (PoW) to Proof of Stake (PoS), no longer requiring GPU mining. Millions of graphics cards flooded into the second-hand market, and prices plummeted. This batch of GPUs, whose lifespans had been drained by mining rigs, ironically became "cheap ammunition" for AI training—some startup teams built training clusters with second-hand mining cards, completing early model experiments at extremely low cost.

The flow of computing power is like water: it flows wherever there is profit. From mining to AI training, the destiny of GPUs is rearranged by global capital.

Winter 2019. I accompanied a friend who runs an e-commerce business to Hangzhou for a Taobao merchant conference. In the venue, the host repeatedly emphasized "traffic is money." My friend whispered to me: "Last year, I burned 300,000 yuan on Direct Train, and profit was only 150,000 yuan. We're not selling goods; we're working for the platform." At that moment, I understood what collecting rent means.

In 2020, I helped a friend who makes handmade leather goods open a store on Taobao. Three months later, he gave me a calculation: for every 200-yuan wallet sold, Direct Train burned 40 yuan, platform commission deducted 10 yuan, packaging and logistics 20 yuan—only 130 yuan left in hand—while leather and labor costs were 100 yuan. He said with a wry smile: "Not selling goods, but working for the platform." At that moment, I understood: the so-called platform is essentially a landlord. Rent collection has begun.

Around 2018, the issue of e-commerce platform commissions attracted widespread attention.

Opening a small shop on Taobao and selling some handicrafts—this was the choice of many individual entrepreneurs at the time. But opening a shop is easy; making money is hard. Taobao doesn't charge store opening fees, but it charges various fees: Direct Train ads cost a few cents to a few yuan per click, with conversion rates of only a few percent; Display Ads charge by impression, hundreds of yuan per day; store decoration templates cost hundreds of yuan per year; data analysis tools cost thousands of yuan per year.

Do the math: for every 100 yuan in goods sold, about 20 yuan is paid to Taobao and various service providers. If you add time costs, you hardly make any money.

This is the reason many sellers complain: Taobao is no longer a fair market but a rent-collecting empire.

This also explains why Pinduoduo could rise. Pinduoduo doesn't charge commissions, so sellers can lower prices and attract price-sensitive users.

The rent collection behavior of platforms has an ethical problem.

Platforms originally existed to reduce transaction costs. They connect buyers and sellers, provide trust mechanisms, and improve transaction efficiency. This is creating value.

But when platforms become monopolists, they begin to extract value. They increase commissions, force sellers to buy ads, and squeeze riders and drivers. This is distributing value, not creating value.

How much rent should platforms collect? This is a question with no standard answer. Collect too little, and the platform cannot be profitable or sustain service provision. Collect too much, and sellers and buyers are squeezed, and the market loses vitality.

Platforms should be regulated. The government should limit platform commission rates and protect the interests of sellers and buyers. The government should encourage competition and break monopolies.

But regulation also has costs. Excessive regulation may stifle innovation and hinder the emergence of new platforms.

This is a problem that requires balance. And balance requires wisdom.

**Epilogue: April 10, 2021**

Alibaba was fined 18.2 billion yuan.

This is the largest fine in China's antitrust history. The State Administration for Market Regulation determined that Alibaba has a dominant position in China's online retail platform service market and abused its market dominance by imposing "choose one of two" requirements on merchants, excluding and restricting competition.

"Choose one of two" means: if a merchant opens a store on Alibaba's platform, they cannot open a store on other platforms. This is typical monopolistic behavior.

Alibaba stated that it accepts the penalty and will rectify.

This is a turning point in the era of platform rent collection. The government is beginning to realize the impact of platform monopolies on society and is starting to intervene.

But fines only treat the symptoms. The real cure is to encourage competition and give the market more choices.

**Five years later, 2028**: Decentralized e-commerce platforms based on blockchain technology mature; merchants can trade directly with consumers without paying platform commissions. The commission model of traditional platforms faces fundamental challenges.

**Ten years later, 2033**: AI Agents become the new entry point; users no longer open e-commerce platforms but directly tell AI "help me buy a shirt." The value of platforms shifts from "traffic entrance" to "supply chain service," and commission rates drop significantly.

**Thirty years later, 2053**: When 3D printing and local manufacturing become widespread, goods no longer need to be transported from afar; does the concept of "platform" still exist? When AI can directly coordinate producers and consumers, do we still need "middlemen"?

The destiny of code is ultimately determined by rules.

And the next chapter of the story has just begun.
