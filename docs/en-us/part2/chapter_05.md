# Chapter 5: That Piece of Glass Without a Keyboard

January 9, 2007. San Francisco.

The air at the Moscone Center was thick with anticipation and coffee. Thousands packed the venue—tech journalists, analysts, Apple employees, and devoted fans who had flown in from around the world. They knew that whenever Steve Jobs said "One more thing..." there would be a surprise.

Fifty-two-year-old Jobs stood on stage. Black turtleneck—custom-made by Issey Miyake, he had bought hundreds at once; blue jeans—Levi's 501, he wore them his whole life; gray New Balance sneakers. This outfit, he wore from 1998 to 2011, never changing.

"I don't want to waste energy deciding what to wear," he once said.

But today, he had much more to say than just clothing.

"Today, Apple is going to reinvent the phone."

His speaking style was famously theatrical. Pauses, movement, eye contact. He wasn't reading from a script; he was performing. Every gesture, every pause, meticulously designed.

He pulled a device from his pocket.

It was a piece of glass. No keyboard, no stylus. Just a circular Home button.

"This is not three devices," he said. "This is one device."

He swiped his finger to unlock the screen. Tapped an icon to open Safari. Pinched to zoom in on a webpage. Rotated the phone, and the screen automatically rotated.

The audience erupted.

This was the iPhone. A computer in your pocket, a terminal connected to the internet, a device covered in sensors.

On June 29, 2007, the iPhone launched in the United States. People lined up overnight; Apple stores were packed.

The first-generation iPhone had many flaws: no 3G network, only 2G; no App Store, only pre-installed apps; no copy and paste; non-replaceable battery; priced at $499.

But it changed everything.

Old phones were dead.

---

**Meanwhile, on the other side of the Earth.**

2007, Google headquarters in Silicon Valley.

Andy Rubin stood in a conference room, facing a group of Google executives. He was demonstrating a mobile operating system.

"Android," he said. "Open source. Any manufacturer can use it."

Rubin had founded Android Inc. in 2003, aiming to develop a smartphone operating system. In 2005, Google acquired him. Now, under Google's protection, he was challenging Apple.

Google's strategy was clear: search was the core, mobile search was the future growth point. If the iPhone dominated the mobile market, Apple might exclude Google Search. Google needed a backup plan—an open mobile operating system.

Android's design philosophy was completely different from iOS:

Open. Open source, any manufacturer could use it.

Diverse. Supported various hardware configurations, from low-end to high-end.

Free. Allowed users to install third-party app stores.

In October 2008, the first Android phone, the HTC Dream, launched. It had a sliding physical keyboard, a trackball, and a not-so-smooth operating system.

But it was a start.

---

The iPhone was not the first smartphone.

Before it, there was Nokia's Symbian, BlackBerry's BlackBerry OS, and Microsoft's Windows Mobile; Palm's webOS would not arrive until 2009.

But the iPhone was the first truly "smart" smartphone.

The secret was that piece of glass.

Before the iPhone, most phone touchscreens were resistive screens. Two layers of film that required forceful pressing. Didn't support multi-touch, could only recognize one touch point.

The iPhone used a capacitive screen. Utilizing the body's electrical conductivity: when a finger touched the screen, it changed the electric field distribution on the screen. No force needed; a light touch would respond. And it could detect multiple touch points simultaneously.

Multi-touch made "pinch to zoom" possible. Two fingers spreading or pinching on the screen, and the image zoomed in or out. This interaction was so natural that users didn't need to learn how to use it.

Inside the iPhone were several tiny sensors:

Accelerometer, measuring device acceleration. When the phone was rotated, it detected changes in gravity direction and notified the operating system to rotate the screen.

Proximity sensor, detecting distance between device and objects. When the phone was held to the ear for a call, it automatically turned off the screen to prevent accidental touches.

Ambient light sensor, detecting ambient light intensity. In bright light, the screen automatically brightened; in dim light, it automatically dimmed.

The original iPhone did not include a gyroscope; precise angular-rate sensing became standard with the iPhone 4 in 2010.

These sensors allowed the phone to "perceive" the environment and user behavior. The phone was no longer a passive device but an active participant.

---

The iPhone ran iOS.

Initially, iOS only allowed one app to run in the foreground. When users pressed the Home button to exit an app, the app was terminated. Later, iOS introduced background tasks but still strictly limited resource usage for background apps.

iOS's interface design was touch-centric. Buttons were large enough for finger tapping; swipe operations were smooth with millisecond-level response times; animation effects were natural, giving users physical world feedback.

The first-generation iPhone had no App Store. Apple pre-installed several apps: Phone, Messages, Safari, Mail, iPod, Photos, Camera.

Developers who wanted to develop apps for the iPhone could only make web apps. Jobs at the time believed web apps were sufficient.

Developers didn't think so. They began "jailbreaking" iPhones to install third-party apps.

Apple realized that native apps were necessary. In July 2008, the App Store launched.

Developers paid $99 per year. Apps had to pass Apple's review. 30% of sales revenue went to Apple, 70% to developers.

In July 2008, when the App Store launched, there were only 500 apps. A year later, over 100,000. By 2020, over 2 million apps, with cumulative downloads exceeding 200 billion.

The App Store pioneered a new business model: the platform provides infrastructure, developers provide content, and the platform and developers share revenue.

More developers meant richer apps; richer apps meant more users; more users meant developers were more willing to invest.

This was the manifestation of network effects—product value grows exponentially with the number of users.

---

The competition for mobile operating systems was essentially a competition for app ecosystems.

Users chose which platform based on which had more apps. Developers chose which platform based on which had more users.

This was a classic "chicken and egg" problem.

iOS solved this problem first. The iPhone's user base was high-end with strong willingness to pay; developers were willing to develop apps for iOS.

Android faced greater challenges. Devices were diverse, with varying screen sizes, hardware performance, and system versions. Developers needed to adapt to various devices, with higher development costs.

Google's solution was to lower the development barrier. Android used Java for development, which was easier to learn than iOS's Objective-C.

By 2012, Android's market share exceeded iOS. But iOS still dominated the high-end market and most app revenue.

Two titans standing side by side. The pattern was set.

---

**2010, Zhongguancun, Beijing.**

Forty-year-old Lei Jun founded Xiaomi. Before this, he was CEO of Kingsoft and an angel investor.

He had a dream: to make a smartphone for Chinese people, sell it through the internet, at near-cost prices.

In August 2011, the Xiaomi Phone 1 was released. Priced at 1,999 yuan, its specs rivaled Samsung and HTC phones priced at over 4,000 yuan at the time.

Xiaomi had no offline stores, only sold on its official website. Every Tuesday at noon, sales opened, selling out in minutes.

Lei Jun's speaking style was completely different from Jobs. Jobs was theatrical; Lei Jun was down-to-earth. At the launch event, he said "Are you OK," which netizens turned into a meme video.

But Xiaomi's products were indeed good. The MIUI system updated weekly; user suggestions could be implemented the following week.

By 2014, Xiaomi became China's largest smartphone manufacturer.

Lei Jun said: "Even a pig can fly if it stands in the right wind."

---

The intelligence of smartphones comes not only from software but also from hardware.

Smartphones need to connect to the internet. Connecting to the internet requires baseband chips.

Baseband chips handle mobile communication signals: 2G, 3G, 4G, 5G. They are among the most complex chips.

Qualcomm is the dominant player in the baseband chip market.

Qualcomm's business model is "chips + patent licensing." Phone manufacturers buy Qualcomm chips while also paying patent fees to Qualcomm, even if they don't use Qualcomm chips.

This model is called "no license, no chips." Patent fees are charged at 3%-5% of the phone's total price.

Qualcomm's baseband chips allow smartphones to connect to the internet at high speeds. Without Qualcomm, there would be no prosperity of mobile internet.

---

Inside smartphones are covered with various sensors.

Cameras, from the initial 2 megapixels to later triple and quad camera systems.

GPS, Global Positioning System. Determines the phone's precise location by receiving satellite signals.

Wi-Fi, not only for internet access but also for positioning.

Bluetooth, short-range wireless communication. For connecting headphones, watches, speakers.

NFC, Near Field Communication. For mobile payments.

Fingerprint sensors, for unlocking phones and verifying payments.

Facial recognition sensors, Face ID introduced with iPhone X, using infrared dot projectors and infrared cameras.

These sensors make smartphones the most powerful sensing devices in human history. Every phone is a mobile data collection terminal.

---

Smartphone screens have evolved from LCD to OLED.

LCD uses backlighting; light passes through liquid crystal layers and color filters. Advantages are low cost and long lifespan; disadvantages are low contrast and limited viewing angles.

OLED doesn't need backlighting; each pixel emits light on its own. Advantages are high contrast, wide viewing angles, and flexibility; disadvantages are high cost, short lifespan, and burn-in.

In 2017, iPhone X first used an OLED screen. Since then, high-end iPhones have used OLED.

OLED screens can be made into flexible screens. Samsung's Edge series has curved screen edges; Huawei's Mate X and Samsung's Galaxy Fold have foldable screens.

Summer 2010, Zhongguancun, Beijing.

I pulled the newly bought iPhone 3GS from my pocket and used two fingers to zoom in on a photo for the first time.

The moment my fingertip touched the glass, that smooth feedback stunned me—this wasn't operating a machine, this was having a conversation with a piece of living glass.

Before that, I used a Nokia N73, with keys, a joystick, and a resistive screen. Every click required forceful pressing.

At that moment, I knew: the old world was over.

---

The sensors in smartphones make them the devices that know users best.

GPS knows where you are, the accelerometer knows if you're moving, the camera knows what you see, the microphone knows what you hear, the contacts know who you've contacted, payments know what you've bought.

This data knows users better than users know themselves.

Internet companies keenly perceived this. They developed apps, collected data, analyzed behavior, and pushed ads.

Users enjoyed free services, paying with their privacy.

In 2013, Snowden exposed the US "PRISM" program: the US National Security Agency collected user data on a massive scale through companies like Apple, Google, and Facebook.

This exposure sparked global concern about privacy.

Privacy and convenience are an eternal trade-off. Users want personalized services but don't want to be monitored. Tech companies want data but don't want to be accused of privacy violations.

Smartphones gave humans unprecedented capabilities and tech companies unprecedented power.

---

**October 5, 2011**, Steve Jobs passed away.

After an eight-year battle with pancreatic cancer, he died at his home in Palo Alto, California, at age 56.

Apple headquarters flew flags at half-mast. Apple stores around the world had flowers and notes placed outside.

Jobs didn't see the iPhone's peak. When he died, the iPhone 4S had just been released, and Siri had just debuted.

But he saw the beginning of an era.

He changed phones, changed music, changed movies, changed personal computers. He changed the way humans interact with technology.

**Five years later, 2016**: Nearly a decade after the iPhone's birth, global smartphone users exceeded 2 billion. China became the largest smartphone market, with Huawei, Xiaomi, OPPO, and vivo rising.

**Around 2017**: Global smartphone shipments peaked, and the market entered stock competition. Foldable phones commercialized in later years.

**Twenty years later, 2031**: When smartphones are replaced by new computing platforms, when AR glasses and brain-computer interfaces become mainstream, will people still remember that "piece of glass without a keyboard"?

**Thirty years later, 2041**: When computing is everywhere, when humans and machines are deeply integrated, the definition of privacy will be completely rewritten.

The destiny of code depends on the people holding the keyboard.

And the next chapter of the story has just begun.
