# Chapter 1: The Shattered Glass of Netscape and the Faint Light of Submarine Cables

Late autumn 1993. A blizzard had wrapped the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign into an island.

When Marc Andreessen pushed open the door to the NCSA machine room, a gust of air hit him—a mixture of heating, dust, and the acrid smell of overheating circuit boards. He took off his glasses, wiped the fog from the lenses with his sweater sleeve, and put them back on. Only then could he see the dozens of server indicator lights blinking in the darkness, like an ocean of electronic fireflies.

He was twenty-two years old, freshly graduated from the computer science department, yet still lingering at the university, unwilling to leave. His official title was "Research Assistant" at NCSA; in reality, he was a broke student coding day and night. Three months earlier, he had met Eric Bina in this very machine room—a programmer a few years older, married, with a daughter. Bina was the true technical core of the project; Andreessen was the fountain of ideas.

That afternoon, he stood beside Bina's workstation, pointing at the plain-text HTML code on the screen. "Eric, what if we could make web pages display images?"

Bina looked up from the screen, his eyes carrying that expression programmers get when someone pitches a crazy idea: "You mean the `<img>` tag?"

"Yeah, like this." Andreessen grabbed a sheet of printer paper and sketched a diagram. "Add an `<img src='photo.jpg'>` to the HTML, and the browser renders it as an image. Ordinary people don't need to understand code. They just need to see things."

Bina frowned. "Tim Berners-Lee won't like it. He's always insisted the web should be plain text. Images are distracting."

"Tim's an idealist," Andreessen said, his voice carrying the unquestioning confidence of youth, "but ordinary people don't want an internet of only words. They want to see things. They want something like television."

For the next three months, they worked secret overtime. By day, they maintained NCSA's official projects, fixed bugs, replied to user emails. By night, when the machine room held only the two of them, they worked on the `<img>` tag.

Now, the code was finally running.

Andreessen walked to his own workstation and peeled off his snow-dampened coat. The monitor was still on, its blue-white glow conspicuous in the dim machine room. He took a deep breath and typed the test command on the keyboard. In the browser window, a test image slowly loaded—the University of Illinois logo, the orange-and-blue Illinois logo, striking against the black background.

The moment the image finished loading, Andreessen felt an electric current shoot up his spine.

Before that moment, web pages had only text. Hyperlinks were blue underlines that, when clicked, jumped to another page of pure text. The entire internet was like a textbook without illustrations—dry, austere, of interest only to geeks. When Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web in 1990, he insisted it should be academic, serious, plain text. Images? Too frivolous.

But Andreessen knew Tim was wrong.

"Eric," he called out, his voice echoing in the empty machine room. "Come look at this."

Bina emerged from behind another row of servers and stared at the screen. His face was expressionless, but Andreessen could see the light in his eyes.

"It actually works," Bina said, a hint of surprise in his voice.

"Not just works," Andreessen said, his eyes gleaming. "It changes the game."

He clicked the mouse and opened another test page in the browser. This time, it was a landscape photo—the cornfields of Champaign, golden stalks glittering in the sunset. The photo loaded slowly. At 28.8K modem speeds of only about 3KB per second, this 80KB photo took over twenty seconds to fully render, line by line scrolling down the screen. But when it finally appeared, the impact made Andreessen forget the anxiety of waiting.

This was the future. This was the future of the internet. Not academic materials in plain text, but multimedia for the masses.

"We need to release this," Andreessen said.

"Will the university approve?" Bina asked.

"Screw it," Andreessen said, a sly smile playing at the corner of his mouth. "We'll release it first and ask for forgiveness later."

In January 1993, Mosaic was released to the public with the `<img>` tag. Downloads exploded within a week—from a few hundred to a few thousand, then tens of thousands. NCSA's servers were crushed by the traffic. People were desperate for this browser that could display images.

Andreessen stood by the window of the machine room, looking out at the campus blurred by the blizzard. He knew his life was about to change. In early 1994, he left NCSA and went to Silicon Valley.

---

April 1994. Mountain View.

Jim Clark sat in a corner of a restaurant, a cup of cold coffee in front of him. He was forty-nine years old and had just resigned from Silicon Graphics. SGI was a company he had founded with his own hands, once worth tens of billions of dollars, specializing in high-end graphics workstations. Hollywood's special effects, from *Jurassic Park* to *Terminator 2*, were all done on SGI machines.

But SGI was declining. The graphics performance of personal computers was getting better and better, and SGI's expensive workstations were losing their market. Clark had clashed with the board and resigned in a fit of anger. Now, with several million dollars in hand, he was looking for the next big opportunity.

He read an article about the Mosaic browser—two young men from the University of Illinois had written a browser that could display images on web pages, and downloads had already exceeded one million. Clark emailed Andreessen.

"Marc, this is Jim Clark. I saw the browser you made. I want to meet you."

Three days later, Andreessen flew to Silicon Valley. They met at a restaurant in Palo Alto. Andreessen wore jeans and a T-shirt, looking like a college student—in fact, he was a college student. Clark wore a casual suit, his hair already starting to gray, but his eyes were still sharp.

"Tell me," Clark said, "can this thing make money?"

Andreessen smiled. This was the question he had been waiting for.

"The browser is free. We sell server software."

"Server software?"

"Yeah," Andreessen said, leaning forward, his eyes gleaming. "Netscape Navigator, free download. Anyone can download it from the website, no charge. But businesses need server software to build websites—Netscape Enterprise Server, that's what we charge for. Five thousand dollars per server."

Clark stared at Andreessen for a long time. He saw something in this young man—that fanaticism unique to entrepreneurs, that absolute confidence in technology, that certainty of "I know what the future looks like."

"How old are you?"

"Twenty-two."

"Do you know how to run a company?"

"No," Andreessen said honestly, "but I know browsers will change the internet. I know that in five years, everyone will be using browsers to go online. I know this is the next big opportunity."

Clark was silent for a moment. He remembered when he had founded SGI, also in his early thirties, also full of passion. Now, twenty years later, he was once again standing at the starting line of entrepreneurship.

"I'll invest," he said. "You bring the technical team. We'll split it fifty-fifty."

Andreessen nearly jumped out of his chair.

On April 4, 1994, Netscape Communications Corporation was officially established in Mountain View. Clark was CEO, responsible for financing and business. Andreessen was Vice President of Technology, responsible for product. They poached Eric Bina from the University of Illinois, along with other members of the Mosaic team.

They rewrote a browser from scratch—Navigator. They didn't use Mosaic's code because the copyright belonged to the University of Illinois. Starting from zero, they wrote a faster, more stable engine. The engine was called Mozilla—Mosaic + Godzilla, meaning "Mosaic Killer."

---

October 13, 1994. Navigator 1.0 was released.

That morning, Andreessen and Bina stood guard in Netscape's server machine room. The servers were rented, located in a data center in Silicon Valley. Hundreds of machines were lined up in the room, the noise of the fans like a plane taking off. Andreessen stared at the monitoring screen, his palms sweating.

"Uploaded," Bina said, pressing Enter.

The Navigator installation file was pushed to the download server. Andreessen refreshed the webpage and saw the download link was live.

"Now what?" Bina asked.

"Wait," Andreessen said. "Wait for the world to download."

They waited ten minutes. The download count was zero.

"Maybe nobody's interested," Bina said, a hint of nervousness in his voice.

"Wait a bit more," Andreessen said.

At the twentieth minute, the download count started to rise. A dozen times, two dozen times, then suddenly spiking to several hundred.

"The servers can't handle it," Bina said, staring at the monitoring screen, his face changing. "Bandwidth is maxed out."

"Add bandwidth," Andreessen said. "Quick!"

Bina called the data center and urgently requested more bandwidth. But the traffic was rising too fast; the servers were struggling on the edge of collapse. By the end of that day, Navigator had been downloaded over ten thousand times. The next day, fifty thousand. The third day, one hundred thousand.

By the spring of 1995, Navigator held 90% of the browser market.

The door to the internet had been kicked open.

But this was not a victory of technology; it was a victory of network effects. The more Navigator users there were, the more willing developers were to build web pages for it; the more web pages there were, the more willing users were to use Navigator. Product value grew exponentially with the number of users—this is the scale network effect described by Metcalfe's Law. When a product's value grows exponentially with its user base, first-mover advantage is everything. Netscape's 90% market share was not only a victory of technology but also a victory of scale network effects.

---

Meanwhile, on the other side of the Earth.

In April 1994, China achieved full Internet connectivity through the NCFC project. Later, in 1995, a company called InfoHighway in Beijing's Zhongguancun began offering public dial-up access. Zhang Shuxin, the company's founder, stood in the machine room, watching technicians debug the equipment. The servers were imported from the United States, shockingly expensive. The modems were Hayes, several hundred dollars each.

"What's the bandwidth?" Zhang Shuxin asked.

"64K," the technician said. "Compared to the US, it's as slow as a snail."

Zhang Shuxin was silent for a moment. She knew that China's internet journey was just beginning. No submarine fiber-optic cables, no Cisco routers, no Wintel alliance. But what she didn't know was that on the other side of the Pacific Ocean, a twenty-two-year-old young man had just changed everything.

The `<img>` tag. Images. The internet had crossed from the era of text into the era of multimedia.

And this world would never look back.
