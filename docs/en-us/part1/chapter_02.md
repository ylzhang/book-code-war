# Chapter 2: The Uh-Oh and the Penguin's Cry

Summer 1996. The heat in Tel Aviv, Israel, hit like a wall.

Four young men crowded into an office without air conditioning, the noise of computer fans mixing with the cicadas outside the window. Yair Goldfinger stared at the crude interface on the screen, his finger hovering over the Enter key. His T-shirt was already soaked with sweat, sticking to his back.

"Ready?" he asked.

Arik Vardi nodded, eyes fixed on the screen: "Send it."

Goldfinger pressed Enter. The upload completed. ICQ—I Seek You—was officially released.

No one imagined that this simple program would change the way a billion people communicated in the coming years. Even less did anyone imagine that the iconic "uh-oh" would become a collective memory of the internet age.

---

The birth of ICQ was born of chance.

In early 1996, Goldfinger and Vardi were chatting in a café in Tel Aviv. They were both programmers, both pondering the same question: The internet had email, newsgroups, chat rooms—but why wasn't there a tool that let you know when your friends were online?

"Imagine," Goldfinger said, eyes gleaming, his coffee cup frozen in mid-air, "you turn on your computer and see a list of all your friends. Who's online, who's offline, clear at a glance. You double-click a name and can chat instantly."

"Like caller ID for phones," Vardi said, "but for the internet."

"Exactly! And when a friend comes online, your computer makes a sound."

"What sound?"

Goldfinger thought for a moment, then imitated a sound: "Uh-oh!"

They laughed. Uh-oh. It sounded like someone suddenly discovering something, or like the surprise of an old friend suddenly appearing. This sound later became ICQ's trademark, a memory of youth for a generation.

This was ICQ's core concept—giving the internet "presence." You knew who was online and who was offline. You were no longer sending messages into the void, but to specific people. The internet transformed from a lonely ocean of information into a bustling social square.

---

November 1996. The first version of ICQ was released.

Downloads grew slowly. The first week, a few thousand people. The first month, tens of thousands. Goldfinger began to wonder if the idea was wrong.

But then, viral spread began.

A college student in New York downloaded ICQ and invited his roommate to use it. The roommate invited his friends, who invited their friends. The theory of six degrees of separation was perfectly validated on the internet—it took an average of only six forwards for a message to reach anyone in the world.

By June 1997, ICQ had over 1 million registered users.

By 1998, over 10 million.

That "uh-oh" echoed through internet cafés, dormitories, and offices around the world. It meant someone was thinking of you, someone was online waiting for you, someone wanted to talk to you. Before this, the internet was lonely. You browsed web pages, read news, sent emails—but you were alone. ICQ gave the internet social connection, warmth, humanity.

In 1998, America Online acquired ICQ for $287 million. The four Israeli founders became multimillionaires overnight. At the signing ceremony, Goldfinger said to Vardi: "We changed the world." Vardi nodded: "Yes, but we don't yet know what that means."

---

**Meanwhile, on the other side of the Earth.**

Late autumn 1998. Shenzhen Huaqiangbei.

Room 402, Building 2, Seg Technology Park—an office less than 100 square meters. Boxes of instant noodles were stacked in the corner, and the air was thick with the mixed smell of cheap cigarettes and coffee. Five desks were crammed together, computer screens emitting blue-white light like the eyes of five ghosts.

Pony Ma sat at one of the desks, staring at the OICQ code on the screen. He was 27 years old, wearing glasses, thin, silent. His university classmate Zhang Zhidong sat across from him, debugging the server. Zeng Liqing, Xu Chenye, Chen Yidan—all five founders of Tencent were present.

"Pony," Zhang Zhidong suddenly said, his voice tired, "the server crashed again."

Ma rubbed his temples: "How many times today?"

"Third time."

"User growth is too fast," Ma said. "Our servers can't handle it."

OICQ had been live for only nine months, and registered users had already exceeded 1 million. This was faster than ICQ. But fast meant trouble—server costs were skyrocketing, while revenue was zero.

Tencent had only a few tens of thousands of yuan left in its account. Paying salaries was becoming a problem.

Ma stood up and walked to the window. Outside was the night view of Huaqiangbei, neon lights flashing, voices bubbling with life. This was the distribution center for China's electronic components, the frontier of reform and opening up. Every day, people became rich overnight here, and others went bankrupt overnight.

He remembered the decision from a few months ago.

---

Early 1998. Shenzhen.

Ma stood in the corridor of Seg Technology Park, holding a fax in his hand. It was from America Online, about ICQ. He showed the fax to Zhang Zhidong.

"This thing is huge in Israel," Ma said. "AOL spent $287 million to acquire it."

Zhang Zhidong's eyes widened: "$287 million? Dollars?"

"Yes."

"Can we make one too?"

Ma was silent. Their current business was making pager software for telecom operators, but the pager market was shrinking. SMS was rising. They needed to find a new direction.

"ICQ doesn't have a Chinese version," Ma said. "Servers are in the US, slow for Chinese users. And it's all in English—ordinary people can't understand it at all."

"So we make a Chinese version?"

Ma nodded: "OICQ. Open ICQ."
