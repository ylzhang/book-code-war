# Preface: Why I Wrote This Book

## I. Confessions of a Chronicler

I wrote this book because I cannot stop thinking.

I am not a historian, nor a financial pundit. I am someone who has slogged through code and data, a chronicler who refuses to linger on the surface.

In 2009, when I wrote my first line of code, the browser wars had already ended, and the mobile internet was blazing ahead. I missed the clamor of Netscape's IPO, the shock of the iPhone's unveiling, and the frenzy of Google's IPO. When I entered the industry, those stories were already legends.

But I was fortunate enough to become a participant, a beneficiary, and an observer of this era.

I worked at top-tier internet companies in China, where bosses returning from Silicon Valley brought back not just technology, but an engineering culture: a faith in code, an obsession with rapid iteration, and the ambition to change the world. After 2015, I began traveling frequently between Silicon Valley, New York, and Boston—attending Y Combinator's Demo Day, visiting NVIDIA's headquarters, and listening to Jerry Yang recount Yahoo's glory days at Stanford. In 2024, I had the privilege of experiencing OpenAI up close as a visitor—the AI Lab permeated with a dense engineering culture and a geek spirit, while the Starbucks downstairs was packed with entrepreneurs in heated discussions. I was overwhelmed with emotion, yet also felt the执着 and anxiety those engineers held for AGI.

I am not someone who stands atop the waves. I never had the Bell Labs internship that Dr. Kai-Fu Lee had, nor did I witness the glory and decline of AT&T. But like Wu Xiaobo chronicling *The Turbulent Thirty Years*—I did not start a business in the waves, but I understand them, record them, and tell their stories.

The protagonists of the stories in this book are Lei Jun, Steve Jobs, Jeff Bezos, Jack Ma, Jensen Huang, Liang Wenfeng... It is they who wrote the code, defined the architectures, and made the decisions. I am merely the chronicler, piecing their stories into a panorama, restoring snippets of code into battles of war.

This is the stance of this book: I am not the protagonist, but I am a faithful chronicler.

## II. Why These Thirty Years?

Because these thirty years represent the most violent technological transformation in human history.

In the long river of human civilization, thirty years is but a flick of the finger. But these thirty years were enough to rewrite the rules by which the world operates.

**The First Rewrite: The Flow of Information**

From 1994 to 2005, browsers tore down the walls of geography. Information began to flow like water, no longer constrained by national borders, language, or time.

Before that, knowledge was locked in libraries, in the minds of experts, in the archives of institutions. To understand a concept, you needed to go to the library and flip through books; to buy a product, you needed to visit a store and pick it out; to meet someone, you needed an introduction from a friend.

The browser changed everything. It liberated information from physical carriers, allowing it to flow through fiber-optic cables. Yahoo organized this information with human-curated directories, Google searched it with algorithms, and Amazon and eBay monetized it through trading platforms.

**The Second Rewrite: The Dispatch of the Physical**

From 2006 to 2015, smartphones inserted sensors into the pockets of all humanity. Platforms were no longer just connecting information; they began to dispatch the physical world.

The iPhone was the beginning of it all. It was not a phone; it was a global network of nerve endings. GPS positioning, accelerometers, gyroscopes, cameras—every phone was sending real-time data on location, behavior, and preferences to the cloud.

Uber used this data to dispatch vehicles, Airbnb to match rooms, and Meituan to calculate the optimal routes for delivery riders. Two-sided platforms evolved from "information matching" to "physical fulfillment."

**The Third Rewrite: The Codification of Intent**

From 2022 to the present, large models have turned intent into code. You no longer need to learn how to use software; you simply say what you want.

This is the ultimate disruption of two-sided platforms. In the traditional model, users needed to search, browse, click, and compare to find the services they needed. Platforms acted as intermediaries, matching supply and demand and extracting commissions.

But in the intent-driven network, AI directly understands the user's intent and completes all operations. The intermediary is bypassed. Platforms must either evolve or die.

## III. Why Two-Sided Markets?

Because two-sided markets are the master key to understanding internet business models.

It took me a long time to understand this.

At first, I thought internet companies were technology companies. They wrote code, built products, and sold services. Later, I realized this understanding was wrong.

The essence of internet companies is not technology, but platforms. They connect two or more groups, facilitate interactions between them, and extract value.

**Google** connects search users and advertisers. Users search for free; advertisers pay for placement. The more accurate Google's search algorithm, the more users are willing to use it; the more users, the more advertisers are willing to pay. This is a classic two-sided network effect.

**Taobao** connects buyers and sellers. The more buyers, the more sellers are willing to set up shop; the more sellers, the more buyers are willing to use it. As a platform, Taobao extracts commissions from transactions and earns revenue from advertising.

**Didi** connects passengers and drivers. The more passengers, the more drivers are willing to take orders; the more drivers, the more passengers are willing to use it. Didi extracts a commission from every transaction.

What these platforms have in common is that they all underwent a metamorphosis from "dragonslayers" to "dragons."

Initially, they broke old intermediaries, reduced transaction costs, and created value. Users loved them because they made life more convenient; merchants loved them because they brought traffic.

But when they became monopolists, they began to collect rent. They increased their take rates, forced merchants to buy ads, and squeezed riders and drivers. Users and merchants became "tenants" of the platforms.

This is the destiny of two-sided markets: starting with the elimination of information asymmetry, and ending with the creation of new information barriers.

## IV. Why the China-US Dual-Core?

Because this is not just a story of technology, but also a story of geopolitics.

In the first two decades of the internet, the United States was the absolute leader. Browsers, search engines, social networks, smartphone operating systems—all these foundational inventions came from Silicon Valley.

China, in that phase, was mainly a follower and a copycat. Tencent modeled ICQ to create OICQ, Baidu modeled Google to create Chinese search, and Alibaba modeled eBay to create Taobao. This is not shameful; it is a fact.

But in the mobile internet era, China began to catch up.

Didi defeated Uber China in the subsidy wars, Meituan established dominance in local services through the "thousand-group wars," and ByteDance disrupted information distribution with recommendation algorithms. Chinese companies were no longer just copying; they began to innovate.

By the AI era, the world had moved toward a dual-core structure.

The United States builds gods in the cloud. OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic possess the world's most powerful large models. NVIDIA possesses the world's most powerful AI chips. America holds the "brain" of AI.

China refines gold in the mud. BYD and CATL possess the world's most powerful electric vehicle supply chains. DJI and Unitree possess highly cost-effective robotics manufacturing capabilities. China holds the "body" of AI.

This dual-core structure is the result of the interplay between technological evolution and geopolitics. It is not temporary; it is long-term.

## V. Why the Intertwining of Software and Hardware?

Because software cannot exist without hardware.

The deeper my understanding of this industry, the more profoundly I understand this point.

Browsers need fiber-optic cables to transmit data, search engines need servers to index web pages, large models need GPUs to train and infer, and robots need motors to move.

Behind every line of code, there is hardware supporting it.

But the story of hardware is often overlooked.

People talk about Google's search algorithm, but rarely about the millions of servers that support search. People talk about ChatGPT's intelligence, but rarely about the tens of thousands of GPUs consumed in its training. People talk about the future of autonomous driving, but rarely about the cost of LiDAR and onboard chips.

This book will tell the story of hardware.

I will tell how Cisco's routers laid the physical foundation of the internet, how Qualcomm's baseband chips made data flow through the air, how TSMC and ASML hold the physical limits of silicon-based civilization, and how NVIDIA evolved from gaming graphics cards to the computing overlord of the AI era.

Software is the visible thread; hardware is the invisible thread. They twist and rise like a DNA double helix, jointly writing these thirty years.

## VI. Who Is This For?

**First, technology practitioners.**

If you are a programmer, product manager, or entrepreneur, this book will help you understand the underlying logic of your industry. Every line of code you write, every product decision you make, is embedded in this grand historical process. Understanding this process will help you make better judgments.

**Second, investors and business decision-makers.**

If you want to understand the business essence of the technology industry, this book will provide a framework. Two-sided market theory, network effects, platform rent-seeking, the China-US dual-core—these concepts will help you see the moats and risk points of technology companies.

**Third, general readers interested in the history of technology.**

If you are simply curious about what happened in these thirty years, this book will give you a panoramic view. I do not assume you have a technical background; I explain complex concepts in accessible language and string together dry history with stories.

## VII. A Final Word

This book is my answer sheet for these thirty years of technological history.

It is not perfect. It will inevitably have omissions, biases, and errors. History is complex, and any narrative is a simplification. I can only guarantee that I have strived to be objective, truthful, and insightful.

But I hope it can help readers understand this era.

We are standing at a crossroads. AI is reshaping everything: work, life, society, geopolitics. No one knows what the future will hold.

But we can learn from history. We can look at how browsers tore down the walls of information, how smartphones dispatched the physical world, and how large models codified human intent.

History does not repeat, but it rhymes.

Understanding the past is the only way to better face the future.
