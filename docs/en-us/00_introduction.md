# Introduction: To the Nameless Code That Changed the World and the Burning GPUs

In 1993, at the National Center for Supercomputing Applications (NCSA) at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, Marc Andreessen and Eric Bina made Mosaic the first browser to display images reliably on the web. Outside the window was the biting wind of the Midwestern plains; inside the server room, only the hum of cooling fans. He didn't know that the `<img>` tag was about to tear a fissure in an old world.

Twenty-eight years later, on November 30, 2022, ChatGPT officially went live. Server clusters roared to life in data centers across Oregon, and NVIDIA A100 chips spiked to 80 degrees Celsius. Within five days, one million users flooded in.

Two pieces of code. Two moments. Thirty years between them.

In those thirty years, the world was rewritten three times.

**The first time**, browsers shredded the walls of geography. Information began to flow like water. Yahoo built the first lighthouse of the internet with human-curated directories. Amazon turned books into bits from a garage. eBay built trust between strangers. Silicon Valley invented the protocols and standards, and the world followed. In that era, China was still on dial-up, and the screech of 14.4K or 28.8K modems was the era's most jarring background noise.

**The second time**, smartphones inserted sensors into the pockets of all humanity. On January 9, 2007, Steve Jobs stood on the stage at Macworld and pulled a slab of glass without a keyboard from an envelope. The iPhone was not a phone; it was a global network of nerve endings. GPS positioning, accelerometers, gyroscopes, cameras—every phone was sending real-time data on location, behavior, and preferences to the cloud. Uber used this data to dispatch vehicles. Airbnb used it to match rooms. Meituan used it to calculate the optimal routes for delivery riders. Two-sided platforms evolved from "connecting information" to "dispatching the physical world." This time, China was no longer just following. Didi fought Uber street by street. Meituan fought its way out of the "thousand-group wars." A more extreme environment, denser populations, and more frenzied capital pushed the O2O model to limits Silicon Valley had never imagined.

**The third time**, large models turned intent into code. You no longer needed to learn how to use software; you simply said what you wanted. Perplexity is dismantling the bilateral matching of classical search. ChatGPT is devouring the traffic entry points of content platforms. Intermediaries are being murdered. And this time, the world has split into a dual-core: the United States builds gods in the cloud, commanding the API network of large models; China refines gold in the mud, using precision manufacturing and extreme supply chains to control the physical torso of embodied intelligence.

This book is a record of these thirty years of the symphony between software and hardware.

What I want to tell is not the glamorous IPO bells and market cap curves. What I want to tell is the underlying code, the distributed databases, the high-concurrency trading systems, and the fiber-optic cables buried under the ocean and the burning GPUs that support those trillion-dollar valuations.

What I want to tell is how two-sided markets evolved from dragonslayers that eliminated information asymmetry into monopolistic empires that collect rent.

What I want to tell is how software and hardware twist and rise like a DNA double helix—behind every great application stands a chip company, a communication standard, a semiconductor fab.

What I want to tell is how Silicon Valley led absolutely for the first two decades, and how China caught up in the next decade through engineering and supply chains, ultimately forming a dual-core structure in the AI era.

This is not a book that celebrates the victors. This is a book that dissects the corpses.

Because only by seeing clearly the dead code, the bankrupt companies, and the obsolete technical routes can we understand why OpenAI and not Google rules the large model era, why NVIDIA and not Intel holds the hegemony of computing power, and why ByteDance and not Facebook invented the recommendation algorithm-driven short video.

Every line of code has its destiny. Every GPU has its temperature.

Let us begin from that cold winter night in 1994.

That was the sound of Netscape's glass shattering.
