# Chapter 12: The Super Terminal That Grew Four Wheels

2018. Fremont, California. Tesla Factory.

Elon Musk slept on the factory floor.

It was the most difficult moment of the Model 3 production hell. Tesla had promised to produce 5,000 Model 3s per week, but production line problems were constant, and capacity was far below target.

Musk moved into the factory, supervising production day and night. He slept on a table in the conference room and showered in the restroom. He fired incompetent managers and personally adjusted the production line layout.

In June 2018, Tesla finally achieved its production target of 5,000 Model 3s per week. Musk posted a photo on Twitter: him sleeping on the factory floor, next to a pile of documents.

This photo became a symbol of Musk's work style: extreme, paranoid, uncompromising.

The success of the Model 3 marked the arrival of the electric vehicle era. It proved that electric vehicles could be better, cheaper, and more popular than gasoline cars.

The era of gasoline cars was over.

**Meanwhile, on the other side of the Earth.**

1995. Shenzhen.

Wang Chuanfu founded BYD, initially making nickel-cadmium and lithium-ion batteries that supplied Nokia, Motorola, and other phone makers.

In 2003, BYD acquired Qinchuan Automobile and entered the automotive industry. Wang Chuanfu's judgment was: electric vehicles are the future, batteries are the core of electric vehicles, and BYD has battery technology that can be transformed into car manufacturing.

This judgment was considered crazy at the time. The electric vehicle market in 2003 was almost zero. Toyota's Prius was a hybrid, not a pure electric. Tesla was founded the same year.

But Wang Chuanfu persisted. BYD launched the F3DM, a plug-in hybrid car. It was not successful, but it accumulated experience.

In 2015, BYD launched the Tang, a plug-in hybrid SUV. It achieved success in the Chinese market, becoming the best-selling new energy SUV.

By 2020, BYD sold about 430,000 new energy vehicles, ranking among the global leaders.

What is a smart car?

It is a computer on wheels.

The core of an electric vehicle is the battery. Battery performance determines the electric vehicle's range, charging speed, safety, and cost.

Range: The larger the battery capacity, the longer the range. But the heavier the battery, the heavier the vehicle, and the higher the energy consumption. This is a balance problem.

Charging speed: The higher the charging power, the faster the charging. But high-power charging generates a lot of heat, requiring an efficient cooling system.

Safety: Lithium batteries may catch fire when overcharged, over-discharged, or impacted. The battery management system needs to monitor the status of each cell to prevent accidents.

Cost: The battery is the most expensive component of an electric vehicle, accounting for 30%-40% of the total vehicle cost. Reducing battery costs is the key to lowering electric vehicle prices.

Battery technology evolution:

Lithium iron phosphate: Good safety, low cost, low energy density. BYD's Blade Battery is an innovation in lithium iron phosphate batteries, improving energy density and safety by changing the cell structure.

Ternary lithium: High energy density, high cost, slightly lower safety. Tesla used ternary cells on premium models and adopted LFP widely on standard-range cars from 2021; CATL is a leading supplier of both LFP and ternary chemistries.

Solid-state batteries: Use solid electrolytes, with higher energy density and better safety. But the technology is not yet mature, and mass production timing is uncertain.

By 2023, battery costs have dropped to around $100 per kilowatt-hour. The purchase cost of electric vehicles is approaching that of gasoline cars.

---

The motor of an electric vehicle is much simpler than the engine of a gasoline car.

A gasoline car's engine has hundreds of moving parts: pistons, crankshafts, valves, camshafts... each part can fail.

An electric vehicle's motor has only one moving part: the rotor. Simple structure, low failure rate, low maintenance cost.

Types of motors:

Permanent magnet synchronous motor: High efficiency, small size, high cost. Most electric vehicles use permanent magnet synchronous motors.

Induction asynchronous motor: Slightly lower efficiency, larger size, lower cost. Tesla used induction motors in its early days, then switched to permanent magnet synchronous motors.

Motor control requires precise electronic control systems. The inverter converts the battery's DC power to the AC power needed by the motor, and the controller adjusts the motor output based on the accelerator pedal position.

---

The core competitiveness of smart cars is autonomous driving.

Autonomous driving is divided into six levels:

L0: No automation. The driver fully controls the vehicle.

L1: Driving assistance. The vehicle can control steering or acceleration/braking, but not both at the same time.

L2: Partial automation. The vehicle can control steering and acceleration/braking simultaneously, but the driver needs to monitor the environment.

L3: Conditional automation. The vehicle can drive autonomously under specific conditions, but the driver needs to be ready to take over at any time.

L4: High automation. The vehicle can drive completely autonomously within a specific area without a driver.

L5: Full automation. The vehicle can drive completely autonomously under any conditions.

By 2023, most smart cars are at the L2 level. Tesla's FSD, XPeng's NGP, and NIO's NOP are all L2-level assisted driving systems.

---

The hardware foundation of autonomous driving includes:

Cameras: Used to identify traffic signs, lane lines, vehicles, pedestrians. Tesla mainly relies on cameras, using 8 cameras to cover 360 degrees.

LiDAR: Emits laser beams and receives reflected signals to generate 3D point clouds. High precision but expensive. Waymo and Baidu Apollo use LiDAR.

Millimeter-wave radar: Detects obstacles and measures distance and speed. Low cost but low resolution.

Ultrasonic radar: Used for parking assistance and close-range obstacle detection. Very low cost.

Computing platform: Processes data from sensors and runs autonomous driving algorithms. NVIDIA's Drive platform and Tesla's FSD chip are the main solutions.

---

The software algorithm of autonomous driving includes:

Perception: Identifies objects in the environment, such as vehicles, pedestrians, and traffic signs. Deep learning models are used to process images and point cloud data.

Localization: Determines the vehicle's precise location. High-precision maps and GPS are combined for positioning.

Planning: Plans the driving path based on the target and environmental information. Path planning algorithms consider safety, efficiency, and comfort.

Control: Controls the vehicle's steering, acceleration, and braking to follow the planned path. Control algorithms need to respond in real-time with millisecond-level precision.

---

The smart cockpit is another important feature of smart cars.

The smart cockpit includes:

Large central control screen: Replaces traditional buttons and knobs for controlling vehicle functions. Tesla's central control screen is 15 inches; BYD's rotating screen can switch between horizontal and vertical.

Voice interaction: Users control vehicle functions through voice commands. Xiao Ai, NOMI, and Xmart are all voice assistants.

In-car entertainment: Supports music, video, and gaming. Tesla's in-car system can play games; BYD's DiLink system can install apps.

OTA upgrades: Vehicle software is upgraded over the air, continuously adding new features. Tesla frequently pushes OTA upgrades to add autonomous driving features and entertainment functions.

In 2020, I first rode in a Tesla Model 3.

The salesperson turned on Autopilot, and the vehicle automatically followed the car in front, maintaining lane position. My hands were on the steering wheel, but the vehicle was actually driving itself.

That feeling was magical—like the vehicle had its own brain.

But I also noticed its limitations. When encountering road construction, Autopilot exited; when encountering sudden lane changes by other vehicles, it braked abruptly. It's not truly autonomous driving, but assisted driving.

True autonomous driving is still far away.

**Epilogue: 2026**

Tesla released the Cybercab, a fully autonomous taxi.

It has no steering wheel, no pedals, only a cabin. Users hail a ride through an app, the vehicle arrives automatically, and users get in and arrive at their destination.

This is Musk's vision: when vehicles don't need drivers, the cost of ride-hailing will drop to one-tenth of what it is now.

But this vision faces challenges. Regulatory approval, technical maturity, user acceptance—all are unknowns.

The destiny of code depends on the people holding the keyboard.

And the next chapter of the story has just begun.
